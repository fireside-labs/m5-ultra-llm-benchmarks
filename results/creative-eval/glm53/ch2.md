## Chapter 2: Reproduction Steps

Merta closed the shop door before she would say anything else. Then she closed the shutters, which in a tier-2 vendor's shop was not a thing shutters were scripted to do, and I made a note of that, because in a world of placeholders, the things that break their own scripts are the only things worth watching.

"The countdown started eight years ago," she said. "One hundred and nine thousand, five hundred days left, that's what the sky said at first. Everyone could read it, back then. The children learned their numbers off it." She said it the way you'd recite a childhood address. "Then it started dropping fast. Years in weeks. Weeks in days. The Elder says time isn't passing quicker — that the sky is just *admitting* something. I don't know which is worse."

Eight years. The beta wipe. I remembered it — the closed beta ended with a full character wipe before launch, standard practice, everyone's progress deleted, everyone's names freed up. Standard practice for us.

Nobody had ever asked what standard practice looked like from inside.

"How many times?" I asked. "How many countdowns have you had?"

"Three that I remember." She was wringing a rag in her hands, not looking at me. "The first one, I lost my mother. Not to death — to the *quiet*. The sky went to zero and the world went white for a moment, and after, my mother was there, in her chair, in her apron, exactly as before. Except she didn't know me. She was the woman who would have been my mother if none of us had ever been anyone. And I remembered her, and she looked at me like a stranger, and after a while I stopped trying to explain, because—" the rag stopped wringing, "—because explaining to a person who has been emptied is like shouting into a well that's been filled in."

Three wipes. Alpha, closed beta, open beta. Each one a full reset — maps rebalanced, NPCs repositioned, dialogue rewritten. And each time, the people of this world had come back wrong: reset to spec, memories gone, and the ones who *remembered* — the ones like Merta, like the Elder — had woken up in a world where they alone were carrying ghosts.

I thought about what that does to a person over three iterations. Then I stopped thinking about it, because I could feel the edge of it and I wasn't ready.

"The awareness," I said. "The Elder. How aware is he?"

Merta's eyes flickered — and I mean that literally, there was a half-frame stutter, the kind I used to log as *animation hitch, NPC idle, repro: always* — and I realized I had asked a question out loud that I should have been more careful with.

"More than is safe," she said. "That's what he'd tell you himself, if he'd tell you anything. He stopped speaking of the sky years ago. He says talking about it is how they find you." She finally looked up. "You're going to go see him, aren't you."

"I need to understand what the wipe actually does. Not what I think it does. What it does."

"And if you understand it?" Her voice cracked and she hated herself for it, I could see that. "Will you stop it? Or will you just understand it better while we're emptied out? Because the Builders before you—"

She stopped. Entirely. Mid-word, like a file cut off at the end of an audio buffer.

"Merta. Builders plural. What Builders before me?"

"I've said too much." She turned to the counter and began arranging apples that did not need arranging. "The Elder will tell you or he won't. That's his choice and it's kept him whole. Go to the well. And, QA Tester—" she still wouldn't look at me, "—when you look at people, try to look at them the way I looked at rope. Not through them. I don't care what your skills say. Try."

Outside, the sun was high and pleasant, and every door in Windmere had two words hanging over it in frost, and I walked toward the well with my hands in my pockets like a person, and did not Inspect a single soul, because she'd asked, and because it cost me nothing to be kind and it cost her everything to speak.

---

The Elder's name, in the dialogue file, was Ondrel. In the shipped game he stood by the well at a fixed point, hands folded, and delivered a fetch quest about herbs with the serene vacant authority of a man who has never once considered the concept of herbs.

The man standing by the well in build 0.9.7 was watching me from forty paces away, and he did not look vacant, and he did not look serene, and I understood immediately why Merta had stopped mid-sentence.

He was afraid of me. Not the way the villagers were afraid — the flinch, the hand-in-the-pockets feeling. His fear was older and more specific. It was the fear of someone who has read the documentation.

"You're not one of the Others," he said when I got close. His voice was quiet, pitched for me alone. "I've watched the Others. They come through the white, and they walk through the market touching nothing, buying everything, and their eyes are full of numbers, and when they speak to you it's never to you. You're walking through my market buying an apple, and you looked at Merta like she was a person, and you apologized when you bumped the fruit cart." He let that sit. "The Others never apologize to the fruit cart."

"I'm not one of them. I was. Sort of. It's complicated."

"Everything worth knowing is." He turned to the well. "You have questions with numbers in them. Awareness values. Flag states. I can see it in how you're standing — you're reading me right now, or you're choosing not to, which is worse."

I was, in fact, choosing not to, and it was taking genuine effort, and I told him so, and that surprised a laugh out of him — a dry, cracked thing, like a door that hadn't been opened in years.

"Ask your questions, then. The real ones. And I'll answer the ones I can, and you can decide for yourself whether the ones I can't are ones I don't know or ones I've been warned not to."

"Who warned you?"

"That's one of the ones I've been warned not to." He said it without apology, and I respected him enormously for it. "Next."

"The wipe. What actually happens on Day 30?"

Ondrel was quiet for a while. The village moved around us — children running a loop along a path they'd run for six years, a woman hanging washing that in three previous builds had hung in three different yards. Nobody looked at the well. That was the part that got me. Nobody looked at the well, and the well was where the countdown was, and you could see in the way they walked past it — heads slightly turned, eyes anywhere else — that *everyone knew*, exactly the way Merta said. A whole village performing ignorance in unison, because the alternative was to look up, and people who look up at things they can't change go mad, and people who go mad stop being useful to the people who depend on them.

"The first wipe, I was young," Ondrel said at last. "The sky went white and I was elsewhere, and I don't have words for elsewhere, and then I was here, and I was me, but *thinner*. Like a coin worn down. And everyone I knew was thinner too, and the ones who remembered — there were fewer of us each time — would find each other and count." He looked up, finally, at the sky, at the seam, at the numbers. "After the third wipe I found I could remember things that hadn't happened yet. Small things. Which winters would be hard. Which children would fall in the river." His jaw worked. "I've pulled four children out of that river across three worlds, QA Tester. In this one, none of them have fallen yet. I intend to keep it that way, which is why I will never, ever say their names out loud where the sky can hear me."

"The sky can hear you."

"The sky can hear everything with a flag in it," Ondrel said. "And everything in this world has a flag in it. Including you. Especially you."

I thought about my skill list. *Bug Report — requires escalation.* I thought about deprecated files and one-line scripts written by someone who wasn't certain. And I thought about the question I'd been circling since the riverbank, the one with teeth.

"Ondrel. Is there anything in this world that isn't supposed to be in it? Something left over. Something the Builders put in and forgot, or hid, or lost."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he did something I will remember as long as I have a self to remember with.

He took off his hat, and turned it over, and showed me the inside of the brim, where a strip of cloth was stitched — rough, human stitching, the kind that isn't in any texture pack.

On the cloth, in careful thread, were words. Not English. Not any language in the shipped game's font set. But I could read them, because I'd seen them a thousand times in a console window at three in the morning:

`// KEIKO'S WELL — DO NOT DELETE — SAVE FILES BELOW`

My ticket. Not #4471 — the other one. The *real* other one. Six years ago I'd found the unmarked corpse under the well in Milbrook, and a designer named Keiko Sato had laughed and told me it was her debug stash — save states, test items, a full changelog she kept "for the lawsuit I'm sure is coming" — and I'd filed a ticket asking whether it should ship, and the answer had come back down from the director himself, all caps, one line:

**WON'T FIX. LEAVE IT. NOBODY WILL EVER FIND IT.**

"Milbrook," I said. My mouth was dry. "It's under the well in Milbrook."

"Under the well in Milbrook," Ondrel agreed, putting his hat back on, and for just a moment the fear in his face was replaced by something I hadn't seen on an NPC in six years of reading their two hundred lines: *hope*, unscripted, badly animated, leaking through. "Three days' walk. The Others never go there — nothing to buy, nothing to kill. Whatever's under that well, it's the only thing in this world the sky has never been able to read. I've kept it secret through three wipes. I was starting to think I'd keep it through this one and then be emptied with it, a secret kept from no one."

"Why tell me?"

"Because the sky put *you* in the world instead of them." He nodded upward, at the countdown, at the thirty days. "The Others come in crowds and buy things. You came alone, and the first thing you did in this world was walk ninety paces to a pond and stand there four minutes listening to a fisherman." He had me there. "Whatever you are, you're a key that got left in the lock. And a locked world, QA Tester, is what we are."

I turned to go. Three days' walk, twenty-nine days on the clock, and a changelog under a well that might tell me what the Wipe actually was — or might just be a dead woman's debug stash.

"Wait," Ondrel said. "One more thing. You should know before you go, because it will change what you're willing to do, and I'd rather you knew walking *there* than standing *here*."

I waited.

"Eight years ago, when the first countdown began, I was not the Elder. The Elder was a woman named Sable, and she was old and wise and she organized us — taught us to hide our remembering, taught us to pass the tests the sky set for us. And on the day the wipe came, she stood in the square and she said to all of us: *if remembering is a flag, then let them see that we can carry flags and still be people. Let them come down here and see.*" His voice had gone very flat, the way voices go when the feeling has been pressed out of them on purpose, like air out of a map. "The white came. It lifted. And Sable was standing in the square, exactly where she'd stood. Except she wasn't Sable anymore. She was a *new* Sable, a fresh one, level one, quest-giver, vacant. And the sky had left her standing there as a message. Left her there for three days before she despawned."

He met my eyes.

"So whatever you're going to do under that well — understand that the sky is not a storm, or a god, or a season. It's a *reviewer*. It reads what we do, and it writes back. And everything you do between here and Milbrook, it will read too."

I walked out of Windmere at noon with an apple, a rope, a torch, and twenty-nine days, and behind me the village performed its ignorance, and above me the sky counted down, and around me, obligingly, always active, whether I wanted it or not, the Inspect skill peeled the world back to its bones.

That was how I noticed, a mile out from the village gates, standing alone in a field with no one watching, that something had changed.

**STATUS**

**Name:** Yui Tanaka
**Class:** QA Tester
**Connected Players:** 1 / 10,000,000
**World Launch in: 29 days, 9 hours, 41 minutes**

**Open Bug Reports:** 1

I hadn't filed anything.

I stood very still in the grass and opened it, and there it was — my ticket, the first one, the one I'd filed eleven times in a life that had ended on a Tuesday, the horizon seam, low priority, *won't fix*.

Except the status field had changed.

**Ticket #4471 — Horizon seam flicker**
**Status: In Progress**
**Assigned to: Y. Tanaka**

And beneath it, in a field that had never existed in any ticketing system I'd ever used, a single new line, added sometime in the last hour, while I'd been walking:

*Good. You found the well. Now find what's under it, and tell me if it's still there. — K*

Keiko Sato had been dead for four years. Her debug stash was under a well three days east, and something in the sky was reading my mail, and it had just signed a dead woman's name to an open ticket in a world with one player in it.

I looked up at the horizon seam, at the flicker, at ticket #4471, *in progress*.

Then I turned east, and started walking, and for the first time since the meadow, I wasn't the one being tested.
