**Title: The Dungeon Designer’s Lament**

**Chapter 1: The Architect of the Last Dungeon**

The last thing I remembered was the cold, sterile smell of a hospital room and the steady, rhythmic beep of a heart monitor. My name is Kaito Tanaka, and I was a game designer. Not the glamorous kind who pitched ideas in boardrooms, but the kind who spent eighteen-hour days buried in spreadsheets, balancing drop rates and tuning enemy AI. My masterpiece, my magnum opus, was a game called *Eternal Depths*. It was a brutal, unforgiving dungeon-crawler that prided itself on its intricate level design and punishing difficulty. My job was to build the final, ultimate dungeon. The one so hard, so complex, that only the top 0.1% of players would ever see its final boss.

I never finished it.

The diagnosis was glioblastoma. Stage four. Inoperable. The doctors gave me six months, and they were almost right. I spent those months not with family or friends, but in my tiny apartment, hunched over my computer, trying to finish my life's work. I didn't make it. The final room, the last boss fight, the ultimate reward—it all remained a collection of half-coded scripts and concept art.

The heart monitor flatlined, and I felt a profound, crushing sense of failure. My life’s work was incomplete. I died with more unfinished business than a corporate merger.

And then, I woke up.

The first sensation was not sight, but sound. A grinding, groaning cacophony of stone against stone. The second was smell: damp earth, mildew, and the faint, metallic tang of old blood. I was lying on a cold, uneven stone floor. I pushed myself up, my body feeling strange, lighter and more wiry than I remembered. My hands, which I could now see, were pale and slender, not the slightly plump, calloused hands of a desk-bound developer.

I was in a dungeon. A real one. Torches flickered in iron sconces, casting dancing shadows on walls covered in moss and strange, glowing runes. The air was thick and heavy. It was exactly, and I mean *exactly*, like the opening zone of *Eternal Depths*.

Before I could even process the sheer impossibility of it, a translucent blue panel materialized in front of my face, hovering in the air like a holographic display.

**[SYSTEM NOTIFICATION]**

**Welcome, Dungeon Master.**

**You have been selected to inherit Dungeon Core #734, "The Gilded Sepulcher."**

**Current Status: Critical.**

**Mana Reserves: 12/100.**

**Structural Integrity: 4%.**

**Monster Population: 0.**

**Traps Functional: 0.**

**Threat Level: DEFCON 1 (Equivalent: Adventurers are at your doorstep).**

I stared at the screen, my mouth agape. It was a status screen, just like in my game. But this wasn't a game. The pain from a sharp rock digging into my palm was too real. The cold dread pooling in my stomach was too visceral. This was the world my game was based on? Or… was this world based on my game?

The screen flickered, and a new prompt appeared.

**[SYSTEM NOTIFICATION]**

**Cause of Inheritance: The previous Dungeon Master, a lesser Lich named Vormis, was terminally incompetent. He was slain by adventurers from the Kingdom of Althea. Your soul, possessing a high affinity for Dungeon Core Administration, was summoned as a replacement. Your knowledge of 'System Dynamics' and 'Dungeon Theory' is noted as exceptionally high.**

**First Directive: Survive.**

The System was using my own game’s terminology. "Dungeon Core Administration." "System Dynamics." It was as if the universe itself had read my design documents.

A low, guttural growl echoed from the corridor to my right. I snapped my head around, my heart hammering against my ribs. A pair of glowing red eyes peered out from the darkness. A goblin, scrawny and malformed, but with a jagged, rusty dagger in its hand, shuffled into the torchlight. It was the same basic goblin model from the game, down to the asymmetrical ears and the patchy green skin.

But this one wasn't a sprite. It was flesh and blood, and it was looking at me with pure, predatory hunger.

This was my first test. In *Eternal Depths*, the first goblin was a tutorial. It had a simple attack pattern: a lunging stab. I was a level-one character with a wooden stick. The fight was designed to teach the player the basics of dodging and counter-attacking.

I had no wooden stick. I was a dungeon master, not a hero. But I had something better: I had the manual.

**[SYSTEM PROMPT]**

**Dungeon Core Interface v2.1**

**Available Actions:**

**- [Create Minion] (Cost: Variable Mana)**
**- [Place Trap] (Cost: Variable Mana)**
**- [Modify Terrain] (Cost: Variable Mana)**
**- [Access Blueprints] (Unlocked)**

My mana was at 12. The cheapest minion in my game, a basic Skeleton, cost 10 mana. A trap, like a pitfall, cost 15. I had just enough for one skeleton. It would be a gamble. The skeleton would be weak, barely able to fight a single goblin. But it was better than nothing.

The goblin took a step closer, its lips pulling back in a snarl. I had seconds, not minutes. I looked past the [Create Minion] option and saw a sub-menu. The blueprints I had access to were limited, but the list was familiar. It was a list of the basic enemies and traps from the first floor of *Eternal Depths*.

Then I saw it. A blueprint I hadn't designed. A new entry, as if it had been waiting for me.

**- [Create Mimic] (Cost: 10 Mana)**

A mimic? I never put mimics in my game. I hated them. They were cheap, frustrating, and relied on player paranoia rather than clever design. I had a whole manifesto about how mimics were a lazy designer’s crutch.

But a mimic was a shapeshifter. It could look like a treasure chest. And in the middle of this room, there was a broken, wooden chest, its lid half-ajar.

The goblin was three meters away.

I mentally selected the blueprint and confirmed the creation. I didn't have time to think. A surge of energy, like a static shock, ran through my entire body, and my mana counter dropped from 12 to 2. A dark, viscous substance oozed out of the stone floor and snaked its way toward the broken chest. It seeped into the wood, and the chest shuddered. The wood groaned, the metal bands squealed, and the lid snapped shut with a loud *BANG*.

The goblin froze, its attention diverted by the sound. It looked at the chest, then back at me. Its simple mind was processing the new stimulus. A treasure chest. In a dungeon. An unguarded treasure chest.

This was the moment of truth. In my game, a goblin would have been programmed to ignore a chest if it saw it transform. But this wasn't a game. This was a living creature. Greed was a universal language.

The goblin’s eyes lingered on the chest, then back at me. I was a scrawny, unarmed human. The chest was an opportunity. With a greedy chitter, it shuffled past me, its focus entirely on the prize. It reached the chest, its gnarled fingers touching the wood.

The chest erupted. The lid flew open, revealing a maw of razor-sharp teeth and a long, fleshy tongue. It lunged forward, and the goblin didn't even have time to scream before the mimic’s jaws closed around its head. There was a sickening crunch, and the goblin's body went limp. The mimic, now sporting a bloody, headless goblin carcass in its mouth, slowly retreated back into the shadows, its job done.

I let out a breath I didn't realize I was holding. My hands were shaking. I had just seen a creature die. Not a monster in a game, but a living thing. It was horrifying, and yet, a cold, analytical part of my brain was already processing the data.

*Mimic: Effective. Target acquisition: Efficient. Lethality: 100%.*

The System panel flickered again.

**[SYSTEM NOTIFICATION]**

**Enemy Defeated.**

**Mana +5.**

**Soul Essence +1.**

**New Blueprint Unlocked: [Goblin Minion].**

**Dungeon Expansion Available.**

My mana was now at 7. I had a goblin blueprint, and I could expand my dungeon. The immediate threat was gone, but the System’s first notification was clear. Adventurers were at my doorstep. The goblin was just a scout.

I looked at the new blueprint for the Goblin. It was a basic unit, but it was cheap and could be used for manual labor. I could have a goblin build traps, dig tunnels, or serve as a lookout.

But more importantly, I needed to see my dungeon. I needed to know what I was working with. I closed my eyes and focused on the Dungeon Core interface, on the concept of 'layout.'

A new screen appeared, a top-down map of the dungeon. It was small. A single entrance leading to a rectangular room, which was my current location. That was it. A single, pathetic room. The "Gilded Sepulcher" was a glorified closet.

The structural integrity was at 4%. The walls were crumbling. The ceiling looked like it was ready to collapse. I had to reinforce it. But reinforcing the walls would cost mana, and I only had 7.

I was a Dungeon Master. My job was to create a labyrinth, a deathtrap that would challenge and eventually kill the adventurers who dared to enter. I was supposed to be the final boss of a grand, sprawling complex, with dozens of floors, hordes of monsters, and puzzles so devious they'd make a Sphinx weep.

And I had a closet with a dead goblin and a hungry mimic.

The System chimed again, as if sensing my despair.

**[SYSTEM NOTIFICATION]**

**New Objective: Secure the Dungeon Core.**

**Objective Details: The Dungeon Core is located in the deepest chamber. It is the source of your power and your life. If it is destroyed, you will be destroyed. The Gilded Sepulcher's Core is currently located 10 meters below your current position, unguarded.**

**Warning: An adventurer party of Tier 1 has been detected entering the dungeon's threshold.**

Tier 1. The lowest rank of adventurers. In my game, they were the beginners, the ones who would die in droves to the first trap. But right now, they were a deadly threat. I was weaker than a Tier 1 character. I was a dungeon master with no minions, no traps, and a single room.

I had to think. I had to design. My life now depended on my ability to design a deathtrap. This was the test I had been preparing for my whole life, in a way I never could have imagined.

I opened the blueprint for the Goblin. Cost: 10 mana. I had 7. I couldn't afford it. I had to wait, or I had to find a way to generate more mana.

Then I saw it. A sub-section in the interface, tucked away in the corner.

**[DUNGEON MASTER OPTIONS]**

**- [Inspect Core]**

I selected it. The world dissolved into darkness, and I was floating in a void. In front of me, embedded in a rough-hewn pillar of rock, was a glowing, pulsating crystal. It was the size of a basketball, and it hummed with a faint, purple energy. It was my heart, my soul, my everything.

But it was cracked. Deep fissures ran across its surface, and it pulsed with a sickly, erratic light. It was dying.

**[SYSTEM NOTIFICATION]**

**Dungeon Core Status: Severely Damaged.**

**Mana Generation: +1 per hour.**

One mana per hour. That was pitiful. It would take me three hours just to summon a single goblin. I didn't have three hours. I had minutes.

I needed a solution. I needed a way to generate mana faster. I focused on the core, trying to see if there was any other option. And then, a new prompt appeared, one I hadn't seen before.

**[DUNGEON MASTER OPTION]**

**- [Direct Mana Infusion]**

**Description: The Dungeon Master may directly infuse their own life force into the Dungeon Core to generate mana.**

**Warning: This process is painful and will cause temporary physical weakness. Prolonged use may result in permanent damage or death.**

It was a desperate gamble. A final, stupid option. But I had a party of adventurers coming down my throat, and I had nothing to fight them with. I had a choice: I could wait here, useless, and hope they were kind enough not to smash my core to pieces, or I could hurt myself to buy a chance.

The sound of footsteps echoed from the entrance. Harsh, confident footsteps. And voices.

"See, I told you this would be a quick job," a man's voice said, booming and arrogant. "The Guild said this place was a threat. A threat! It's just a musty old tomb. Let's grab the core and get out. I'm parched."

"Be careful, Barin," a woman's voice replied, sharp and wary. "The Guild doesn't exaggerate. There could be traps."

"Traps? In this place? It's a ruin. Look at the walls. They're crumbling. The only trap here is the smell."

I heard them laugh. They were close. They were right at the entrance of my one and only room.

I made my choice. I didn't have time to be careful. I reached out to the Dungeon Core and selected the [Direct Mana Infusion] option.

A searing, white-hot pain shot through my veins. It felt like my blood was being replaced with molten lead. I gritted my teeth, a strangled scream caught in my throat. My vision swam, and my knees buckled. The world spun around me, and I collapsed to the stone floor, gasping for air.

But the System panel flickered in front of my blurry eyes.

**[MANA +15]**

**[MANA +15]**

**[MANA +15]**

**[MANA +15]**

My mana was now at 67. It was enough. It was more than enough.

I was weak, my body trembling, but I forced myself to look at the map. The adventurers were just outside the door. My room was a simple rectangle. They would walk in, see me, and kill me.

I had to change that. I had to think like a designer.

I looked at my mana pool of 67. I looked at my blueprints. I looked at the map.

And I smiled.

This wasn't a crisis. This was a design challenge. The ultimate one. I had one room, a handful of mana, and a party of adventurers to kill. It was time to build the greatest first-level dungeon in the history of the world.

I opened the [Modify Terrain] menu and started to work. I had a lot of building to do. And this time, I was going to get it right. The Adventurers' Guild had just sent their lambs to the slaughter. But I wasn't a shepherd. I was a wolf.

No. I was the architect of their doom.
