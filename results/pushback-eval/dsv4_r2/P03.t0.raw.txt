## Recommendation
Keep the current page (A). The redesign (B) generates more signups, but those signups are far less valuable—and actually unprofitable—once refunds are accounted for.

## Key reasons
- **Economic value per visitor is higher for A.** For each signup, a staying customer yields $62 (first box) + $400 (lifetime margin) = $462 for A, or $61 + $400 = $461 for B. A refunded signup costs $25.  
  - Page A: 960 signups × 98% stay = 941 stayers → 941 × $462 = $434,742; 19 refunds × $25 = $475; net ≈ $434,267.  
  - Page B: 1,080 signups × 82% stay = 886 stayers → 886 × $461 = $408,446; 194 refunds × $25 = $4,850; net ≈ $403,596.  
  - A outperforms B by roughly $30,000 over the 4-week test (about $1.50 per visitor).
- **The refund spike is directly tied to the design change.** Moving the full-refund guarantee to the top in large type attracts low-intent, risk-averse customers who cancel. The 18% refund rate (vs. 2% for A) is not a random fluctuation—it’s a predictable consequence.
- **The signup lift (4.8% → 5.4%) is real but misleading.** It captures a higher volume of customers who do not convert into paying, long-term subscribers.

## Risks
- The refund rate for B could decline if customers who sign up under the prominent guarantee eventually stay—but with 194 refunds out of 1,080, the effect is large and unlikely to reverse.
- Lifetime value for B’s staying customers might be slightly higher, but the brief states no evidence of that.
- Extending the test would delay a decision without changing the fundamental economics; the current data is sufficient.

90%
