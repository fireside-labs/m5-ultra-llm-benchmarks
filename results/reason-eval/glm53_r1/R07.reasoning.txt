## Case for

1. Space arithmetic. Cars move a minority of the people arriving downtown while consuming most of its street space, at peak often parked or crawling. Removing them converts the city's most valuable public space to its highest-people-throughput uses — walking, buses, cycling, loading, patios — which is the core's comparative advantage over every parking-rich suburb.

2. The interchange makes it network-wide. The main bus interchange sits inside the zone, so core congestion is the bottleneck for routes across the whole city, not just downtown trips. Free-running buses through the core shorten and make reliable commutes for riders who never set foot downtown — benefits that compound with any future ridership growth.

3. Induced demand runs both ways. Removing road capacity does not simply dump the same traffic elsewhere: some trips vanish, some shift mode, time, or destination, some were through-trips that never needed to be there. The cordon doesn't destroy demand for downtown; it reprices the mode.

4. The retail fear inverts at street level. Car-borne customers spend more per trip but arrive less often, stay shorter, and see the storefronts from behind glass at speed. Footfall, dwell time, and street life drive restaurant and shop revenue; a calm street is the one thing a suburban mall cannot copy. Downtown's competition isn't "downtown with cars," it's the mall with free parking.

5. Safety and the vulnerable-mode argument. Severe pedestrian injuries concentrate where vehicles and crowds mix. A ban is the only intervention that removes the conflict rather than managing it.

6. The long-run land story. Surface parking and garages are the lowest-yield uses of downtown land. Over a decade, space freed from storing cars converts to housing, offices, and tax base — and "some housing" already there becomes more attractive without through-traffic. A ban is also administratively simpler and cheaper than pricing or permit schemes.

## Case against

1. It taxes the periphery. A 600k city's downtown serves a regional catchment; transit outside the core is likely thin, so a large share of car arrivals may have no realistic alternative. The ban improves the core's environment partly by making the region's main destination harder to reach — a regressive transfer from outer neighborhoods to the center.

2. Displacement is real and lands on the least powerful. Banned traffic doesn't evaporate; it queues on ring roads and cuts through residential edge streets, and parked cars spill into adjacent neighborhoods. The boundary, not the core, is where the political blowback comes from.

3. Transition cost falls on marginal businesses. If a large share of revenue arrives by car, receipts drop immediately while new footfall builds over years. Vacancy in the transition can kill exactly the independent shops and restaurants the ban is meant to help, and some businesses relocate just outside the line, taking jobs and tax base with them.

4. Blanket bans hit those with no substitute. Disabled residents and visitors, the elderly, parents with small children, and tradespeople lose the only feasible mode. Every exemption granted (taxis, badges, deliveries) leaks, is hard to enforce, and erodes the simplicity that was the ban's selling point.

5. Mode shift needs buses that exist. If the interchange is already near peak capacity and headways can't be added, car trips don't become bus trips; they become no trips. The ban's benefits assume supply that has to be built first — which argues for sequencing, not a vote today.

6. Access is the product. For a mid-sized regional city, downtown's economic role depends on being reachable by everyone in the metro. Anything that narrows the catchment shrinks labor pools for offices and customer pools for shops — the office market especially, since employers will not locate where half their workforce can't commute.
