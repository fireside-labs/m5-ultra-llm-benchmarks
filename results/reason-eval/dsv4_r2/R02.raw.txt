## Missed issues

1. **The headline result is reversed within risk tiers (Simpson's paradox).** Among low-risk patients the program rate is 5.0% vs 4.0% for comparison; among high-risk, 25.0% vs 22.0%. In *both* tiers the program group has the higher rate. The overall 9.0% vs 18.4% exists only because the program group is 80% low-risk while the comparison group is 80% high-risk. If the program group had the comparison group's mix, its rate would be 0.2×5% + 0.8×25% = 21% — worse than the comparison. The report stratified the data and then ignored what the stratification shows.

2. **Enrollment is self-selected, so the comparison is not a control.** Nurses enrolled patients who answered the first call and agreed; the comparison group is everyone else, including non-answerers and decliners. Answering a phone and agreeing to a program correlates with engagement and lower readmission risk. The risk-tier imbalance (80/20 vs 20/80) is direct evidence the groups are not exchangeable.

3. **Dropping 140 non-completers biases the program group upward.** This is per-protocol, not intention-to-treat. Patients who leave a 3-call program often leave because they are readmitted or too sick to continue; removing them inflates the program's apparent success. They should remain in the program group regardless of completion.

4. **The expansion forecast confuses sensitivity with positive predictive value.** Sensitivity 90% does not mean a flagged patient is 90% likely to be readmitted. With 13% prevalence and 70% specificity, PPV = 0.90×0.13 / (0.90×0.13 + 0.30×0.87) ≈ 31%. So 12,000 flagged patients contain ~3,720 actual readmissions, and even a genuine 50% reduction would prevent ~1,860, not 5,400. The forecast is overstated ~3x before questioning the 50% effect itself.

5. **Hospital confounding: Northgate's meds-to-beds service.** 85% of program patients came from Northgate vs 40% of comparison. Northgate launched a bedside medication-delivery program in February 2025 that independently reduces readmissions. The program effect is inseparable from hospital and this concurrent intervention.

6. **Timing confound with a definition change.** 70% of program patients were discharged July–December vs 50% of comparison. On July 1 the measure changed from "any readmission within 30 days" to "unplanned readmission within 30 days," which mechanically lowers counted readmissions. The program group is disproportionately measured under the looser definition.

7. **The Spanish-language "100% reduction" is noise.** 0 of 9 vs 2 of 10 — 19 patients total. A 100% reduction on n=19 is statistically meaningless and cannot support prioritizing Spanish-language nurses.

8. **The balance check is misleading.** Age and sex being similar says nothing when the groups differ massively on the variable that drives readmissions: risk tier (80% vs 20% low-risk). The check reassures leadership while ignoring the one confounder that actually matters.

9. **Excluding deaths within 30 days is a competing-risk bias, not a neutral choice.** If the program (or selection into it) affects mortality, removing deaths from both groups changes the readmission comparison. Symmetric application doesn't make it unbiased if mortality differs.

10. **The forecast's effect and base rate don't come from the pilot.** The forecast applies a 50% reduction (confounded) to a 13% base rate, but the pilot's own comparison rate is 18.4%. Also, sensitivity 90% + specificity 70% implies ~38% of discharges would be flagged, so 12,000 flagged patients implies ~31,700 discharges per year — the scale and transferability assumptions need verification before any expansion math.

## Looks like a problem but isn't

- **Out-of-state readmissions:** the state all-payer claims database captures all in-state hospital readmissions; missing out-of-state stays is a standard, acceptable limitation.
- **First discharge only:** using each patient's first 2025 discharge avoids double-counting the same patient; a reasonable design choice.
- **Excluding deaths:** applied identically to both groups; defensible as a competing-risk simplification, though it can bias if mortality differs (see #9 above).

## Top 3 priorities

1. **Risk-tier confounding (Simpson's paradox):** the stratified data show the program is associated with *higher* readmission rates within both tiers; the headline 9% vs 18.4% is a composition artifact. This destroys the central claim.
2. **Selection and attrition bias:** non-random enrollment plus dropping 140 non-completers means the program group is not comparable to the comparison group; the effect estimate is not causal.
3. **The forecast's PPV error:** the "90% likely to be readmitted" claim is wrong (~31%), so the 5,400 prevented-readmissions projection is overstated ~3x — and it's built on an effect the stratified data contradict.

## Bottom line

The report cannot support the conclusion that the program cuts readmissions in half, and it certainly cannot support expansion. Within every risk tier the program group has a higher readmission rate than the comparison group; the headline result is an artifact of comparing an 80%-low-risk program group against an 80%-high-risk comparison group, compounded by self-selection, per-protocol attrition, hospital mix, and a mid-year definition change. Leadership should not expand. Instead, run a randomized or properly risk-adjusted evaluation using intention-to-treat analysis, correct the forecast math (PPV ≈ 31%), and treat the Spanish-language result as hypothesis-generating only.
