## Case for

1. **Eliminates the congestion externality at the core.** Private cars impose delays on everyone else—buses, pedestrians, and other drivers. Removing them from the 1.5 sq mi core makes the main bus interchange reliable and predictable, which is the single most important asset for a mid-sized city's mobility network. A predictable bus system beats a faster-but-unpredictable car trip for many commuters.

2. **Increases economic yield per square foot of downtown land.** Drivers pass through; pedestrians dwell. Shoppers on foot visit more stores, linger longer, and spend more per visit than those who park, buy one item, and leave. A car-free core converts curb space and parking lots into outdoor seating, plazas, and wider sidewalks—all of which raise the value of the surrounding office and retail space.

3. **Improves safety and public health for the densest population.** The downtown has offices, shops, and housing. Removing cars eliminates the most common cause of severe pedestrian injuries and reduces particulate matter and noise in the area where the most people are concentrated per square mile. This is a direct health benefit to the thousands who live and work there daily.

4. **Forces a virtuous modal shift.** A hard constraint is the most effective way to break car dependence. Once driving downtown is impossible, people will try the bus, bike, or park-and-ride. Increased ridership justifies more frequent bus service, which makes the ban more acceptable, which attracts more riders. This self-reinforcing cycle is the only way to meaningfully change travel behavior in a mid-sized city where car ownership is high.

5. **Reclaims scarce land for higher-value public use.** Parking lots and curb lanes are the most underutilized real estate in any downtown. A ban frees this land for bus lanes, bike parking, green space, or affordable housing—uses that serve far more people per square foot than private car storage.

6. **Reduces the burden on the bus interchange.** The interchange is the choke point. Cars circling for parking and idling at the curb create congestion that delays buses entering and leaving. Removing private cars from the immediate vicinity allows the interchange to operate at its designed capacity, improving the entire regional transit network, not just the downtown.

## Case against

1. **It is regressive and excludes the mobility-impaired.** For the elderly, disabled, and those carrying heavy loads, walking to a bus stop or cycling is not a viable option. A blanket ban on private cars effectively bars these citizens from accessing the downtown's services, shops, and offices. This is an equity problem that disproportionately harms the most vulnerable.

2. **It risks killing the downtown's economic base.** In a mid-sized city, the downtown is often a regional destination—a place people drive to for a specific doctor's appointment, a theater show, or a bulk shopping trip. If the ban makes these trips inconvenient, people will simply go elsewhere (a suburban mall, a different clinic). The loss of car-borne customers may not be offset by increased pedestrian dwell time if the total number of visits drops.

3. **It displaces, rather than solves, the traffic problem.** Cars don't disappear; they just stop at the boundary. The perimeter roads and adjacent residential streets will absorb the through-traffic and parking demand, creating new congestion, noise, and safety hazards in neighborhoods just outside the 1.5 sq mi zone. The problem is shifted, not solved.

4. **The transit alternative may not have the capacity.** The main bus interchange is a bottleneck, not a solution. If the ban forces a 20-30% mode shift to buses, the interchange and the local road network feeding it may not have the spare capacity to handle the surge. Overcrowded buses and long queues at the interchange could make total travel time longer for everyone, including those who switched.

5. **It complicates emergency and service access.** While emergency vehicles are typically exempt, the physical design of a car-free zone—bollards, narrow lanes, restricted curb access—can slow response times for fire and ambulance crews. Similarly, deliveries to shops and restaurants become more complex, potentially increasing costs that are passed on to consumers or reducing the variety of goods available.

6. **It removes flexibility for occasional, non-commuter trips.** A car is the most efficient door-to-door mode for the occasional trip—a family visiting the downtown on a weekend, a parent picking up a child from an after-school activity, a person running multiple errands in one outing. A blanket ban removes this flexibility without offering a comparable alternative, making the downtown a less attractive destination for these important users.

## Cruxes

1. **Empirical: What is the origin and trip purpose of current downtown traffic?** If most trips are short, local, or already by transit, the ban is low-cost and high-benefit. If most trips are long-distance, suburban, single-occupancy vehicle trips for specific errands, the ban is highly disruptive. This is the single most important fact to establish.

2. **Empirical: What is the elasticity of mode shift?** When cars are banned, do people switch to buses/bikes/park-and-ride, or do they simply stop coming to downtown? This depends on the quality of the alternative (bus frequency, park-and-ride capacity, bike infrastructure) and the nature of the trip. If the elasticity is high (people switch), the ban works. If it is low (people stay away), it fails.

3. **Value: How do we weigh individual convenience/access against collective benefits?** This is the crux of the moral disagreement. The collective benefits (cleaner air, safer streets, more public space) are diffuse and long-term. The costs (loss of convenience, exclusion of the mobility-impaired) are concentrated and immediate. There is no empirical answer to this trade-off; it is a question of political and ethical priorities.

4. **Empirical: What is the spare capacity of the bus interchange and the surrounding road network?** Can the transit system absorb a significant mode shift without collapsing? If the interchange is already at capacity, the ban will fail. If it has headroom, the ban can succeed. This is a testable engineering question.

## What evidence would change the answer

- **A 3–6 month pilot program** with temporary street closures in the core, measuring: retail sales (comparing to a control period), footfall counts, bus ridership, and travel times on perimeter roads. This directly tests the displacement and economic impact hypotheses.
- **Origin-destination surveys** of downtown visitors (using license plate data or app-based travel diaries) to establish whether trips are local or regional, and their purpose (commute, errand, leisure).
- **A stress test of the bus interchange**: adding extra frequency during the pilot to see if the network can absorb a 20-30% ridership increase without degrading service.
- **Monitoring emergency response times** (fire, ambulance) to the zone during the pilot, to verify that access for first responders is not materially degraded.
- **Air quality and noise monitoring** (NO2, PM2.5, decibel levels) inside the zone before, during, and after the pilot, to quantify the health benefits.
- **A survey of mobility-impaired residents** to understand the specific barriers the ban creates and whether targeted exemptions (e.g., disabled parking permits) can mitigate them.

## My lean

I lean in favor of the ban, but only as part of a package that includes a substantial upgrade to the bus network and park-and-ride facilities, plus explicit exemptions for disabled access. The presence of the main bus interchange is a key asset that makes the ban viable, and the 1.5 sq mi size is manageable. Confidence: 65%. The main condition is that a pilot must demonstrate that the transit alternative can absorb the shifted trips without a significant increase in total travel time for the majority of users, and that perimeter traffic does not become a worse problem than the original downtown congestion.
