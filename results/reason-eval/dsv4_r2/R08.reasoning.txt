**Case for remote-by-default:**

1. Talent pool and retention — removing geographic constraint expands the labor market dramatically. For a 4,000-person company, hiring and retaining skilled software and finance talent is a core constraint. Remote-by-default is a durable differentiator.

2. Focus and deep work — knowledge work, especially software engineering and finance analysis, requires long uninterrupted blocks. Office environments, especially open plans, fragment attention. Remote gives individuals control over their environment.

3. Autonomy and trust as a signal — the policy change signals trust in professionals. For high-autonomy knowledge workers, being treated as adults who can manage their own time tends to increase engagement and reduce attrition. The "default" matters psychologically: remote-by-default means no one has to justify being away; the burden of justification shifts to in-person.

4. Cost and waste reduction — less real estate, less commuting time, lower carbon footprint. For a company of 4,000, real estate is a big line item. Commuting time is a tax on employees that the company can remove.

5. Asynchronous work as a forcing function — when people aren't co-located, teams are forced to write things down, document decisions, and make processes explicit. This often improves quality of communication and reduces reliance on hallway conversations that exclude people. It can reduce interruptions and improve the quality of the record.

6. Resilience and flexibility — distributed teams are less vulnerable to local disruptions (weather, transit strikes, local events). Also enables global coverage across time zones for a company that may already be distributed.

**Case against:**

1. Tacit knowledge and serendipity — a lot of what a company actually knows is not written down. It lives in informal conversations, overheard discussions, spontaneous problem-solving. Remote-by-default systematically reduces the rate of these encounters. The cost is invisible and accumulates slowly — you don't notice it until a decision is made without context that someone in the room would have supplied.

2. Onboarding and junior development — new hires, especially early-career people, learn by watching others work, by being in the room when decisions are made, by absorbing norms. Remote onboarding is harder; the implicit apprenticeship that happens in offices is hard to replicate. This is a long-run capability cost.

3. Team cohesion and trust under pressure — when things go wrong (a bad quarter, a product crisis), teams need to move fast and trust each other. Trust built in person is more robust. The "default" matters here too: if in-person is the exception, people will under-invest in relationships until a crisis, and then it's too late.

4. Cross-functional coordination — software, finance, and ops need to coordinate constantly. The friction of coordinating across teams is higher remotely; the cost of a misunderstanding is often discovered late. In-person reduces the latency of alignment.

5. The "default" framing cuts both ways — under remote-by-default, teams that do want to meet in person have to justify it and overcome the default. The proposal says "unless their team chooses to meet in person" — that creates a coordination cost for anyone who wants in-person. The path of least resistance is to never meet, and the loss is diffuse. So the policy biases against the very thing that's hard to measure.

6. Career equity and visibility — remote workers, especially those not already well-connected, get less visibility for promotions and stretch assignments. Even if the company intends fairness, proximity bias is a real mechanism. This can create a two-tier workforce: those who happen to be near an office advance faster. This is an equity argument against the policy as much as for it.

**Cruxes:**

The disagreement turns on a few things:

1. How much of the company's critical knowledge is tacit vs. explicit? If most coordination can be made explicit (written, documented, async), remote works. If a large share is tacit and only transferred through informal contact, the office has real value. — This is partly empirical (measurable through e.g. decision quality, onboarding speed) but also a judgment about the nature of the work.

2. What is the actual value of serendipity and informal contact in this specific company? This is the hardest to measure and the crux of most disagreements. Both sides have priors; the evidence is weak.

3. Value question: is the company optimizing for individual productivity and retention, or for organizational cohesion and long-term capability? These can conflict. If you optimize for the individual's focus and flexibility, you may sacrifice collective tacit knowledge. Which matters more is a values question, not just empirical.

4. The default's effect on coordination costs — who bears the burden of arranging in-person? Under remote-by-default, the burden is on the team that wants to meet. That means the policy's real effect depends on how teams actually use the "unless" clause, which is hard to predict.
