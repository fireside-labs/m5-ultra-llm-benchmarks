## Variables

1. **Payer mix and net reimbursement per visit** – This drives financial sustainability. Site B's dense, low-income population likely means high Medicaid/uninsured share (lower reimbursement), while Site A's suburban growth area likely skews commercial. Measure: pull zip-code-level payer mix from state Medicaid claims, commercial insurance penetration, and uninsured rates; model expected revenue per visit for each site.

2. **Unmet demand and service-area volume** – A clinic only works if enough people will actually come. Site A has growth but may be overserved; Site B is dense but may already have safety-net clinics; Site C is near your own assets. Measure: drive-time analysis (15-min radius), primary-care physician-to-population ratio, ED visit rates for non-urgent conditions, and census data on chronic disease prevalence.

3. **Accessibility and transportation** – Urgent care and primary care depend on easy arrival. Site B's bus lines are critical for its population; Site A's highway access serves car owners; Site C's office park may be unwalkable. Measure: transit frequency and headways, parking ratio, walkability index, and average commute time for the target population.

4. **Capital expenditure and operating cost profile** – Site B needs a major renovation (high upfront, likely asbestos/plumbing issues); Site A has low rent but may need build-out for X-ray/lab; Site C is ready. Measure: detailed construction/renovation estimates, rent per sq ft, utilities, maintenance, and property taxes over a 10-year horizon.

5. **Time to operational readiness** – Delay costs revenue and lets competitors entrench. Site C can open in weeks; Site A in months; Site B in a year+. Measure: permitting timelines, construction schedule, and projected opening date; compare against market growth and competitor moves.

6. **Cannibalization and integration with existing assets** – Site C is 2 miles from your hospital and 1 mile from your clinic. This could steal your own patients (lower net new volume) or create referral synergies. Measure: current patient origin data, existing clinic utilization rates (if under 80%, cannibalization is a real risk), and referral patterns.

7. **Competitive landscape** – Site C has a competitor's urgent care across the street, which splits demand and may trigger price/quality competition. Site A/B may have no direct competitor. Measure: map all competing clinics, their hours, payer acceptance, and market share within each site's service area.

8. **Staffing availability and recruitment** – Clinics need physicians, NPs, nurses, and techs. Site C near your hospital can share staff and float coverage; Site B in an older area may face recruitment challenges; Site A in a growing suburb may have a larger labor pool. Measure: local clinician vacancy rates, commute times for current staff, and proximity to residency programs.

9. **Mission alignment and community need** – As a non-profit, you have a mandate to serve the underserved. Site B is the clear mission play. Measure: social vulnerability index, poverty rate, uninsured rate, and access to care gaps (e.g., distance to nearest safety-net provider).

10. **Regulatory and zoning feasibility** – X-ray requires state licensing and radiation safety, lab requires CLIA certification. Site B (former bank) may have vault/plumbing constraints; Site A (retail) may have zoning restrictions on medical use. Measure: zoning code, state health department requirements, and a preliminary structural/utility survey.

11. **Facility suitability for imaging and lab** – X-ray needs lead shielding, power, and space; lab needs water, waste disposal, and ventilation. Site C (office park) may be easiest; Site B (bank) may need major retrofits. Measure: square footage, ceiling height, utility capacity, and a feasibility study for imaging equipment.

12. **Long-term growth and demographic trends** – Site A is in the fastest-growing part of the region, which could mean future volume but also future competition. Site B may be stable or declining. Measure: 10-year population projections, housing starts, age distribution, and employer growth.

## Top 3 priorities

1. **Payer mix and net reimbursement per visit** – Without a sustainable margin, the clinic fails regardless of mission. Site B's likely high Medicaid/uninsured share could mean operating losses, while Site A's commercial mix offers the strongest financial base. This is the first filter.
2. **Unmet demand and service-area volume** – Even with good reimbursement, a clinic needs enough patients. Site A's growth is promising but must be verified; Site B's density may or may not translate to demand. This determines whether the site generates sufficient revenue to cover fixed costs.
3. **Capital expenditure and time to operational readiness** – Site C is ready and cheap to open; Site B is expensive and slow; Site A is in between. For a non-profit with finite capital, a high-cost, slow-to-open site (B) strains cash flow and delays mission impact. This is the feasibility gate.

## What would change my mind

- If Site B's payer mix turns out to be better than assumed (e.g., a recent Medicaid expansion, a large employer with commercial insurance, or a state grant for safety-net clinics), the financial case flips and it could become the top choice.
- If Site A's "fastest-growing" claim is not backed by actual housing starts or commercial development (e.g., it's speculative zoning), then its demand volume collapses, dropping it below Site C.
- If Site C's existing clinic is running at low utilization (e.g., <60%), then opening a new clinic 1 mile away would cannibalize your own volume, making Site C far less attractive than the "ready to occupy" advantage suggests.
- If Site B's renovation cost is within 20% of Site C's ready-to-occupy cost, and the timeline is under 9 months, the mission alignment and dense demand could outweigh the capital hurdle, shifting my top priorities to demand and mission over cost.
