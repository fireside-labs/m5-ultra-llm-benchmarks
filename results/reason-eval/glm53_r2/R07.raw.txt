## Case for

1. **The core's binding constraint is street space, and cars are the least efficient user of it.** A lane moving people by bus or on foot carries far more people per hour than the same lane carrying private cars, and parking is dead land in the most valuable square miles of the city. The ban doesn't remove access — it re-allocates fixed space to the modes most people in a dense core already use.
2. **The bus interchange makes this a regional transit upgrade, not a downtown amenity.** Every route in the metro converges on the core, where buses currently mix with turning, parking, and queuing cars. Removing cars is the cheapest, fastest way to speed up and make reliable the entire network — riders who never enter downtown still gain.
3. **The marginal downtown customer is a pedestrian.** People on foot pass and enter more shops, linger longer, and converting parking to seating directly adds serving capacity for restaurants and cafés. The core's only durable edge over suburban strip retail is atmosphere and density — exactly what car dominance erodes.
4. **Safety and street-level environment improve at the source.** Severe pedestrian injuries come from vehicle conflicts; removing vehicles removes the kinetic energy, the noise, and the exhaust where people actually are — including those who drive to the edge and walk in.
5. **The biggest gains are long-run and compound.** A calmer core attracts housing conversions, small ground-floor businesses, and investment; the first-year debate systematically underweights a five-to-ten-year transformation.
6. **It is cheap and reversible.** Bollards, planters, and camera enforcement cost a fraction of any rebuild and can be undone. The option value favours trying: a failed pilot costs a bad year, not a permanent mistake.

## Case against

1. **In a 600,000-person metro, the core is small and most of its users live where the bus doesn't usefully go.** For car-dependent workers and shoppers, the ban is a price increase on downtown — and their demand can relocate to periphery retail with free parking. The ban doesn't just change modes; it can change destinations.
2. **Sequencing risk: prohibit before alternatives exist and you risk a death spiral.** If bus frequency, edge parking, and loading aren't ready first, access worsens → footfall drops → shops close → footfall drops further. That spiral is far harder to reverse than the ban itself.
3. **Displacement, not disappearance.** Through-traffic and parking demand move to the boundary ring, which gets the congestion and circling while receiving none of the benefits. The council would be exporting the problem to residents who bear costs with no upside.
4. **The core runs on goods and services, not just visitors.** Restaurant supply, construction, trades, waste — every necessary exemption (delivery windows, permits, residents, disability access) adds administration and enforcement burden, and each carve-out erodes the simplicity that makes the ban workable.
5. **A blanket rule is blunt about real differences in need.** Older and disabled people cannot all "park at the edge and walk in" (my assumption: the walk from realistic edge parking is several hundred metres); late-shift workers on sparse bus routes lose out. Permits help but create a two-tier, gatekept downtown.
6. **The upside may be overstated and the costs unpriced.** If most arrivals are already by car, the ban shrinks total arrivals before any mode shift occurs — mode shift is created by alternatives, not prohibition. And plate-recognition enforcement means surveilling every vehicle movement around the core, a cost rarely weighed.

## Cruxes

*Empirical questions:*

1. **Who actually comes downtown, and how?** The person-mode split at the cordon, by purpose (workers, shoppers, residents) — not vehicle counts, which are the classic misleading metric. If a large share already arrives by bus or foot, the ban's cost is low; if most arrive by car from transit-poor areas, it's high. Settled by cordon counts and origin–destination surveys.
2. **Can the alternatives absorb the shift in months, not years?** Spare peak bus capacity, headways, edge park-and-ride, drop-off space. If yes, the "against" case reduces to transition friction; if no, the death-spiral risk is live. Settled by network capacity analysis plus pilot observation.
3. **Is demand destination-elastic?** Do car-dependent users switch modes to keep coming downtown, or switch destinations? Settled by a pilot with before/after footfall, vacancy, and boundary retail indicators.

*Value question:*

4. **What is the downtown for, and who may be charged for the choice?** If the core is primarily a *place* — to dwell, live, and invest — it is legitimate to price out through-movement and accept costs on boundary residents and some mobility-limited people. If it is primarily a regional *node* whose value is maximal access, the ban sacrifices its purpose for its decoration. No evidence settles this; the council must own the distributional choice.

## What evidence would change the answer

- **A 3–6 month camera-enforced pilot** (delivery windows, resident and disability permits) measuring *person*-throughput at the cordon: rises → toward the ban; falls → away.
- **Bus speed and reliability inside the core, and network-wide on-time performance**: clear improvement → toward; no change → the strongest "for" argument weakens badly.
- **Footfall by street and ground-floor vacancy/lease renewals** (revealed behaviour, not petitions or stated intentions, which are noise): footfall up, vacancies flat → toward; closures → away and trigger the sunset clause.
- **Boundary monitoring** — traffic volumes and parking occupancy just outside the zone: roughly unchanged → displacement objection fades; heavy spillover → toward "against."
- **Origin survey of downtown workers**: what share has a feasible transit commute (my assumed threshold: under ~45 minutes door-to-door — the exact number matters less than measuring the share)? High share → toward; low share → away, favouring "alternatives first."
- **A delivery and service-trip audit**: if edge loading plus smaller vehicles can cover daily goods flows at modest cost → toward; if the core genuinely needs heavy vehicle access all day → away.
- **Edge park-and-ride occupancy and drop-off demand**: absorbed without circling or overflow → toward.

## My lean

I lean toward a time-limited, exempted pilot with a pre-committed evaluation and sunset clause — not a permanent ban now — at roughly **60% confidence**. The lean depends mainly on crux 2: if the bus network has spare peak capacity or can add it quickly, the ban is a low-risk bet with regional transit upside; if not, I'd flip to "build the alternatives first, ban second," and my confidence in any ban drops below even.
