## Case for

1. The core's binding constraint is street space, and cars use it inefficiently. A lane moving people by bus or on foot carries far more people per hour than the same lane moving private cars, and parking consumes land that could be patios, loading bays, or wider sidewalks. Banning cars is not removing access; it's re-allocating fixed space to the modes most people in a dense core already use.

2. The bus interchange makes the ban a regional transit upgrade, not a downtown amenity. Every bus entering the core currently mixes with turning, parking, and queuing cars; removing them is the cheapest, fastest way to speed and de-risk the whole radial network. Riders across the metro gain even if they never set foot downtown.

3. Retail mechanism: the marginal customer is a pedestrian. People on foot pass and enter more shops, linger longer, and parking conversion to seating directly adds serving capacity for restaurants. The core's competitive edge over suburban retail is atmosphere and density — precisely what car dominance erodes. (Evidence to settle: footfall and sales in pilot.)

4. Safety and street-level environment: most severe pedestrian injuries come from vehicle conflicts; removing cars cuts kinetic energy at source, plus noise and exhaust at the sidewalk where people actually are. This benefits everyone including people who drive to the edge and walk in.

5. Long-run land value and housing: a calmer core is more attractive to convert offices to housing and to open small businesses; low rents for ground-floor space follow from parking land re-purposing. The biggest gains compound over years and are invisible in the first-year debate.

6. Reversibility and cost: bollards, planters, and camera enforcement cost a tiny fraction of rebuilding anything, and can be undone. The option value favors trying it: the downside of a failed pilot is a bad year, not a permanent mistake.

## Case against

1. In a 600k metro, the core is small; most of its customers and workers live where the bus doesn't usefully go. For them the ban is a price increase on downtown — and their demand can relocate to strip retail with free parking. The ban doesn't just change modes; it can change destinations.

2. Displacement, not disappearance: through-traffic and parked cars move to the boundary ring. Boundary neighborhoods get congestion, circling, and parking spillover without any of the benefits — the council would be exporting the problem to people with no vote on it... well, they do vote. Exporting the problem to residents who get none of the upside.

3. The core runs on goods and services, not just shoppers: restaurant supply, construction, trades, removals, waste. Every exemption (delivery windows, trade permits, disability access, residents) is administratively heavy and enforcement-dependent, and each one erodes the simplicity that makes the ban workable.

4. Equity cuts both ways: car ownership skews... hmm — actually low-income workers may depend on cars if they live off transit corridors; disabled and older people can't easily be served by "park at the edge and walk 800m." A blanket rule is blunt; a permit system creates a two-tier downtown and a bureaucratic gatekeeping.

5. Sequencing risk: if bus frequency, park-and-ride, and loading infrastructure aren't in place first, the transition falls on businesses and workers in the worst retail year... The failure mode is a death spiral: access worsens → footfall drops → shops close → footfall drops further. Recovery from that is far harder than the ban is to reverse.

6. The upside may be overstated: if most people downtown already arrive by car, the ban shrinks total arrivals before any modal shift occurs; mode shift is created by alternatives, not by prohibition. And enforcement via plate-recognition cameras means surveillance of every vehicle movement around the core — a cost rarely priced into the debate.

## Cruxes

Empirical:
1. Who actually comes downtown and how? The person-mode split at the cordon, by trip purpose (workers, shoppers, residents) — not vehicle counts. If a large share already arrives by bus/foot, the ban's cost is low; if most arrive by car from transit-poor areas, it's high. Settled by cordon counts and origin-destination surveys.
2. Can the alternatives absorb the shift within months, not years? Spare peak bus capacity, realistic headways, park-and-ride at the edge, drop-off space. If yes, the "against" case collapses to transition grumbling; if no, the death-spiral risk is live. Settled by network capacity analysis and pilot observation.
3. Is demand destination-elastic? Do car-dependent shoppers/workers substitute to the core by other modes, or substitute the core for somewhere else? Settled by a pilot with before/after footfall, vacancy, and boundary/regional retail indicators.

Value:
4. What is the downtown for, and who bears the cost of choosing? If you believe the core is primarily a place (to dwell, linger, invest) it's legitimate to price out through-movement and accept costs on boundary residents and mobility-limited people; if you believe it's primarily a regional node whose value is maximal access, the ban sacrifices its purpose for its decoration.
