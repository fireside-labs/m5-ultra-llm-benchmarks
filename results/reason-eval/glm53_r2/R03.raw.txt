## Missed issues

1. **The no-show fee is contractually prohibited for the patients who no-show most.** Medicaid managed care is 34% of the panel, and those contracts state the clinic "shall not bill members for missed appointments." Weighting the payer-specific no-show rates (27% / 12% / 11%) by payer mix, Medicaid patients generate roughly 55% of all no-shows (the weighted rates sum to ~17%, reconciling with the stated 18%, so the math holds). The fee's primary target is off-limits. Worse, the billing system applies the fee "automatically" — unless it is coded with payer-level carve-outs (never mentioned), the clinic will bill Medicaid members in violation, risking audit, clawback, or contract termination. The sandbox test proved the mechanism works, not that the policy is lawful.

2. **The January 1 date breaches two commercial contracts.** Two commercial contracts require 90 days' written notice *to the plan* before any new patient-facing fee; the memo provides only 60-day notice to patients and no plan notice at all. Either notices go out immediately and the fee's effective date slips ~90 days for part of the 41% commercial book, or the clinic is in breach on day one.

3. **The $90k fee projection fails three haircuts the clinic's own data supplies.** A $75 fee is a sub-$100 patient-responsibility charge, and the clinic collects only 31% of those. Stack the Medicaid ban (~45% of no-shows remain chargeable) and the automatic first-miss waiver, and plausible net revenue is low five figures, not $90k. (Annual visit volume isn't given, so this is proportional reasoning — but every haircut points the same direction.)

4. **Text-only reminders aim the fine at patients who were never reachable.** 41% lack the opt-in the vendor requires, 15% have no mobile number, and 22% prefer Spanish against English-only templates; overlap unknown, but plausibly near half the panel gets no usable reminder. They then miss appointments and incur a $75 fee they had no fair chance to avoid — and many won't have signed the new financial policy by Jan 1, since signatures happen "at next visit." Expect disputes and waivers concentrated on exactly these patients.

5. **The 18%→8% target has no mechanism behind it.** The top recorded reason is transportation (38%) — fees don't create bus fare, and neither do texts; the highest-no-show group (Medicaid, 27%) can't be fined; and Medicaid managed-care plans typically include non-emergency transportation benefits the memo never considers routing patients to. The fee also improves the number on paper rather than in reality: patients will cancel inside 24 hours (unbillable, slot unfilled) or stop booking — no-shows fall while filled visits fall faster.

6. **The balance hold likely destroys more value than it recovers.** The memo asserts recovery of "about half" of $410k with no basis, while the clinic's demonstrated collection rate on small patient-responsibility balances is 31%. And 9% of >$200-balance patients already leave within 6 months of a collections letter; a hold is a stronger push. At $148/visit, a patient worth even 3–4 visits a year (~$450–600) exceeds the $200 being held for — and balance-carrying patients skew toward high utilizers whose chronic-care visits the hold will block.

7. **The hold's exemption redirects the problem, and the $410k itself is unverified.** Urgent/same-day visits are never held, so balance-carrying patients will either forgo non-urgent care or present as urgent — concentrating the least reliable patients into same-day slots and degrading acute access for everyone else. Separately, the memo never confirms the $410k is adjudicated patient responsibility rather than claims still pending with payers (Medicaid members often owe little or nothing), so holds could be applied to money patients don't owe.

8. **Blanket first-slot double-booking contradicts the fee and worsens lateness.** If the fee works, double-booking guarantees two-show days; if it doesn't, double-booking is a permanent crutch — the two policies can't both be calibrated. With 61% of sessions already running >20 minutes late, and first-of-session slots skewing toward transportation-constrained patients who arrive late even when they come, the predictable result is a queue at minute zero that cascades through the session. Targeted overbooking (by payer, history, day, slot time) or a cancellation waitlist absorbs no-shows with less collateral.

9. **The 30-hours-a-week saving won't survive contact with the policy.** Someone must still call the ~40–50% who can't be texted and the Spanish-preferring patients; curing the 41% opt-in gap is a project of its own; and the fee and hold add new desk work (disputes, waiver decisions, hold conversations at check-in). Re-run the staffing case net of all this.

10. **Chilling effect on a panel that is one-third Medicaid.** The mailed policy reads as a universal $75 fine plus care holds, and patients who can't parse the payer carve-outs — especially the 22% needing Spanish — may simply avoid booking preventive and chronic care. With transportation plus work/childcare explaining 57% of recorded reasons, the clinic risks converting an attendance problem into a missed-care problem, which costs more downstream.

## Looks like a problem but isn't

- Urgent and same-day visits exempt from holds — the clinical safety floor is right (the demand-shifting side effect is listed above, not the exemption itself).
- First-miss waiver plus documented-hardship discretion — a reasonable fairness valve, even though it further shrinks fee revenue.
- The patient-facing notice process (60-day mail, posting, signature at next visit) — adequate on its own terms; the gap is payer-side notice, not patient-side.
- The sandbox-tested automated fee application — the tooling is fine; the flaw is the policy logic fed into it.

## Top 3 priorities

1. **Put the fee through a compliance gate before any approval.** Confirm the scope of the Medicaid prohibition across all MCO contracts, make payer-level carve-out coding in the billing system a precondition, and serve the 90-day notices to the two commercial plans immediately — which almost certainly moves the fee's effective date past January 1. Then decide whether a fee confined to commercial/Medicare no-shows (~45% of the problem) is worth the dispute load at all.
2. **Rebuild the business case from the clinic's own numbers.** Apply the 31% collection rate and first-miss waiver to fee revenue; anchor hold recovery at or below 31% and net out the 9% attrition at $148/visit; attribute the 18%→8% gap to actual causes (38% transportation). The likely conclusion: reminders and transportation routing carry the no-show reduction, and the fee is a rounding error.
3. **Sequence the changes instead of flipping them all on January 1.** Run the opt-in and mobile-capture campaign and add Spanish templates before any text-only switch, keeping calls for the non-textable cohort; replace blanket first-slot double-booking with targeted overbooking or a cancellation waitlist; verify the $410k is adjudicated patient responsibility before any hold goes live.

## Bottom line

Modify — do not approve as drafted. Every line of the expected impact is contradicted by the clinic's own fact sheet: the fee is mostly unchargeable and uncollectable, the hold's economics turn negative once attrition is priced in, and text-only reminders leave roughly half the panel unreachable while fining them. Approve only the groundwork now (opt-in campaign, Spanish templates, payer carve-out coding, plan notices, balance verification) and bring back a rebuilt proposal in 60–90 days.
