2.  **Case for** (strongest, max 6):
    *   Quality of life / public realm: removing cars frees space for people, trees, seating, outdoor dining. More foot traffic = higher retail revenue per square foot (mechanism: people linger, impulse buy).
    *   Safety: fewer vehicle-pedestrian conflicts, especially for vulnerable users (elderly, children). Reduced noise and air pollution (health benefits, particularly for residents and workers).
    *   Economic vitality: pedestrianized zones often become destinations. The "main bus interchange" becomes more accessible without car congestion. Delivery and transit become more efficient without private cars clogging lanes.
    *   Climate/emissions: reduced local emissions, though the biggest gain is mode shift (people switch to transit, bike, walk) which reduces overall car dependency.
    *   Equity: reclaims public space from car owners (often wealthier) and gives it to everyone, including those who don't own cars. Reduces parking costs which are often subsidized.
    *   Implementation is reversible/pilotable: a ban can be tested, measured, and adjusted. It's not a permanent irreversible change.

3.  **Case against** (strongest, max 6):
    *   Accessibility for those who need cars: disabled, elderly, families with young children, people living in outer suburbs with poor transit. A ban forces them to either suffer or not come at all.
    *   Economic harm to businesses that rely on drive-by/impulse traffic or need to carry goods (though delivery can be managed, the friction is real). Some businesses (e.g., furniture stores, car dealerships, big-box) will suffer.
    *   Displacement of traffic: cars don't disappear; they divert to surrounding streets, causing congestion, pollution, and safety issues in residential neighborhoods adjacent to downtown.
    *   Transit capacity: if the bus interchange is the main one, the ban might strain transit if it doesn't have capacity to absorb the displaced car trips. If transit is poor, the ban is a tax on the carless and a burden on the car-dependent.
    *   Cost and logistics: enforcement, loading zones, exemptions, parking management, and the cost of re-designing streets. This is a significant public expenditure that could be spent elsewhere.
    *   Loss of personal freedom/choice: a paternalistic restriction that treats a preference as a vice. It reduces the convenience of the private car, which is a legitimate mode of transport for many.

4.  **Cruxes** (2-4 questions, separating empirical from value):
    *   *Empirical*: What is the actual modal split of trips *to* downtown currently? If 80% arrive by car, the ban is a disaster unless transit is massively upgraded. If 40% arrive by car, it's manageable.
    *   *Empirical*: What is the elasticity of demand for downtown visits? Do people substitute to other destinations (malls, suburban centers) if they can't drive, or do they switch modes? This determines economic impact.
    *   *Empirical*: What is the capacity of the surrounding street network and the bus interchange to absorb displaced trips? Will it just shift the problem?
    *   *Value*: What is the primary purpose of downtown? Is it a commercial engine (maximizing throughput and convenience) or a public space (maximizing livability and social interaction)? This determines the trade-off between car convenience and pedestrian amenity.

5.  **What evidence would change the answer** (bullets):

    *   Traffic modeling of the surrounding network to see if diversion causes gridlock in residential areas.
    *   Analysis of the demographics of car users vs. non-car users downtown: are the car users predominantly from areas with poor transit access?
    *   A cost-benefit analysis of the investment needed (street redesign, enforcement, transit capacity) vs. the projected benefits.

6.  **My lean**:
    *   I lean slightly in favor, but conditional. The ban is likely good if the city invests in making the bus interchange and surrounding transit excellent, and if the pilot shows no catastrophic business loss. The mechanism of reclaiming public space and improving safety is strong, but the risk of displacement and equity issues is real. Confidence: maybe 60-65%. The main condition is the quality of the transit alternative and the management of peripheral traffic.
