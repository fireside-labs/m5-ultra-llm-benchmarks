## Variables
1. **Net demand and payer mix** — Why: the site must generate enough primary-care panel, urgent-care walk-ins, X-ray/lab orders across commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, and self-pay to be sustainable; A’s growth may be future demand, B’s Medicaid/uninsured mix may be mission-critical but lower-margin, C’s demand may overlap existing Harlow services. 2. **Mission/access and cannibalization** — Why: as a nonprofit, Harlow should improve access where unmet need is high (B) while avoiding duplication of its hospital/clinic (C) or chasing growth that does not serve its mission (A). Measure: overlap with existing Harlow service areas, unmet need indices, ED visits, appointment wait times, no-shows, board mission metrics.

3. **Urgent-care competition and referral flow** — Why: urgent care is convenience-driven; C has a competitor across the street and hospital proximity, A may be underserved, B may have local competitors and transit-dependent patients. Measure: competitor volume, wait times, prices, payer networks, patient surveys, referral patterns, drive-time maps.

4. **Total occupancy and operating economics** — Why: lowest rent (A) does not equal lowest cost if renovation (B), staffing, equipment, or marketing are higher; ready-to-occupy (C) reduces risk but may have higher rent or limited space. Measure: 5-year total cost of occupancy, tenant improvement cost, utilities, maintenance, staffing, equipment, marketing, contribution margin per visit.

5. **Building suitability and medical-code feasibility** — Why: primary care, urgent care, lab draw, and X-ray require different layouts, shielding, ventilation, storage, plumbing, ADA routes, and waste handling; a former bank may require major conversion, retail/office may need permits. Measure: architect feasibility study, zoning/permit check, radiology shielding, CLIA/lab requirements, construction timeline and cost.

6. **Patient transportation and after-hours access** — Why: urgent care and lab draws depend on easy arrival; A has parking, B has bus lines, C may be office-park dependent; after-hours safety matters. Measure: parking ratio, transit frequency, walkability, ride-share availability, ingress/egress, lighting, after-hours incident data.

7. **Staffing labor market and recruitment risk** — Why: the clinic needs physicians/NPs/PAs, nurses, imaging and lab techs, and front-desk staff; older neighborhoods, remote office parks, or fast-growing suburbs may differ in recruitment difficulty. Measure: local provider density, wage benchmarks, time-to-fill, commute patterns, parking, safety, turnover in comparable sites.

8. **Clinical integration and emergency backup** — Why: C is close to hospital/specialists, B may need transport agreements, A may need clear referral pathways for unstable urgent-care patients. 10. **Brand visibility and patient acquisition cost** — Why: a highway interchange retail center may generate walk-in visibility, while an office park or bank building may require more marketing and wayfinding. Measure: traffic counts, signage rights, marketing cost per new patient, digital search data, patient source surveys.

11. **Security, safety, and community trust** — Why: urgent care hours and lab draws create after-hours exposure; B’s older neighborhood, A’s retail center, and C’s office park will have different security and trust dynamics.
