

## Variables

1. **Net demand and payer mix** — Why: the site must generate enough primary-care panel, urgent-care walk-ins, X-ray orders, and lab draws across commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, and self-pay patients; A’s growth may be future demand, B’s Medicaid/uninsured mix may be mission-critical but lower-margin, and C’s demand may overlap existing Harlow Valley services. Measure: 10/15-minute drive-time population, payer mix from claims/census, ED utilization, projected growth, contracted rates, and break-even visit volume.

2. **Mission/access and cannibalization** — Why: as a nonprofit, Harlow Valley should improve access where unmet need is high, especially at B, while avoiding duplication of its hospital or existing clinic at C or chasing growth at A that does not serve its mission. Measure: overlap with existing Harlow service areas, unmet-need indices, ED visits, appointment wait times, no-shows, and board mission metrics.

3. **Urgent-care competition and referral flow** — Why: urgent care is convenience-driven; C has a competitor across the street and hospital proximity, A may be underserved, and B may have local competitors and transit-dependent patients. Measure: competitor volume, wait times, prices, payer networks, patient surveys, referral patterns, and drive-time maps.

4. **Total occupancy and operating economics** — Why: lowest rent at A does not equal lowest cost if renovation at B, staffing, equipment, or marketing are higher; ready-to-occupy C reduces risk but may have higher rent or limited space. Measure: 5-year total cost of occupancy, tenant improvement cost, utilities, maintenance, staffing, equipment, marketing, and contribution margin per visit.

5. **Building suitability and medical-code feasibility** — Why: primary care, urgent care, lab draw, and X-ray require different layouts, shielding, ventilation, storage, plumbing, ADA routes, and waste handling; a former bank may require major conversion, while retail/office may need permits. Measure: architect feasibility study, zoning/permit check, radiology shielding, CLIA/lab requirements, and construction timeline and cost.

6. **Patient transportation and after-hours access** — Why: urgent care and lab draws depend on easy arrival; A has parking, B has bus lines, and C may be office-park dependent, while after-hours safety matters. Measure: parking ratio, transit frequency, walkability, ride-share availability, ingress/egress, lighting, and after-hours incident data.

7. **Staffing labor market and recruitment risk** — Why: the clinic needs physicians/NPs/PAs, nurses, imaging and lab techs, and front-desk staff; older neighborhoods, remote office parks, or fast-growing suburbs may differ in recruitment difficulty. Measure: local provider density, wage benchmarks, time-to-fill, commute patterns, parking, safety, and turnover in comparable sites.

8. **Clinical integration and emergency backup** — Why: C is close to hospital/specialists, B may need transport agreements, and A may need clear referral pathways for unstable urgent-care patients. Measure: transfer agreements, ED/hospital response times, EHR connectivity, specialist referral capacity, and transport protocols.

9. **Zoning, licensing, and opening timeline** — Why: the board chooses next quarter, so a ready-to-occupy site may beat a high-potential site delayed by permits, construction, or medical-use approvals. Measure: zoning for medical/urgent care, imaging permits, signage rules, construction schedule, landlord commitments, and certificate-of-need if applicable.

10. **Brand visibility and patient acquisition cost** — Why: a highway interchange retail center may generate walk-in visibility, while an office park or bank building may require more marketing and wayfinding. Measure: traffic counts, signage rights, marketing cost per new patient, digital search data, and patient source surveys.

11. **Security, safety, and community trust** — Why: urgent care hours and lab draws create after-hours exposure; B’s older neighborhood, A’s retail center, and C’s office park will have different security and trust dynamics. Measure: police incident data, local business feedback, security staffing costs, lighting, emergency response times, and community health worker input.

12. **Flexibility, scalability, and exit risk** — Why: A may grow quickly and need expansion, B may need phased renovation, and C may become redundant if hospital/clinic capacity changes. Measure: lease expansion options, adjacent space availability, sublease terms, demographic projections, equipment scalability, and lease break clauses.

## Top 3 priorities

1. **Sustainable demand and payer mix** — the site must attract enough patients and the right payer mix to cover urgent-care, primary-care, imaging, and lab operations without relying on unrealistic growth or unsupported charity care.

2. **Strategic access versus cannibalization** — the site should fill a real access gap, especially for Medicaid/uninsured patients, rather than merely duplicate Harlow Valley’s existing hospital or clinic capacity.

3. **Total cost and buildability** — the site must be affordable and feasible to open next quarter, including renovation, medical buildout, staffing, equipment, permits, and operating losses during ramp-up.

## What would change my mind

- If Site A’s growth corridor already has strong commercial/Medicare demand and urgent-care gaps, while Site B’s renovation cost cannot be offset, I would favor A despite weaker mission fit.

- If Site B’s Medicaid/uninsured population can be supported through grants, FQHC-style funding, Medicaid managed-care contracts, or board mission priorities, I would favor B even with renovation risk.

- If Site C’s competitor across the street is weak—long waits, poor payer network, low quality—and Harlow’s existing clinic/hospital are capacity-constrained, C becomes the fastest, lowest-risk growth play.

- If regulatory or building feasibility makes Site B or Site A impossible to open next quarter, the priority shifts decisively toward ready-to-occupy Site C or the lowest-risk alternative.

- If payer contracting reveals that urgent-care visits require specific site attributes—high traffic, parking, Medicaid acceptance, or in-network status—that only one candidate meets, that variable should dominate the decision.
